1. Source and Acquisition Hardware

All RF experiments use the public DroneRF dataset released by Al-Sa’d et al. (2019) and Allahham et al. (2019) on Mendeley Data. Raw RF activity was captured by two synchronised National Instruments USRP-2943R software-defined radios, each observing one half of the 2.4 GHz ISM band used by the drones’ control and video links.

Receivers
2 × NI USRP-2943R SDR, time-synchronised
Sampling rate
Fs = 100 MS/s, 14-bit ADC
Total bandwidth
80 MHz (two 40 MHz receivers)
Lower band (L)
2.40 – 2.44 GHz, centre 2.422 GHz
Upper band (H)
2.44 – 2.48 GHz, centre 2.462 GHz
Samples per file
10,000,000 raw amplitudes per receiver (one CSV file)
Files
454 CSV files forming 227 synchronised (L, H) pairs
Segment length used
2048 samples = 20.48 µs
Local data paths
data/raw/DroneRF/ (raw), data/splits/ (train/val/test CSVs), data/metadata/dronerf_metadata.csv

2. Class Composition

The four-class identification task distinguishes ambient background RF from three commercial drones. Under the standard benchmark each synchronised (L, H) receiver pair yields 100 segments, giving 22,700 segments in total.

3. Binary Unique Identifier (BUI) Labelling

Each DroneRF recording is identified by a five-bit Binary Unique Identifier [b4 b3 b2 b1 b0]. The three most significant bits (msBUI) encode the drone/experiment type and the two least significant bits (lsBUI) encode the operational mode.

Table 2. BUI encoding.
msBUIMeaninglsBUIFlight mode
000Background RF (no drone)00On and connected to the controller

5. Strict Recording-Level Split

To measure true cross-recording generalisation, the 454 files were partitioned by recording session rather than by segment. The 23 distinct recording sessions are assigned exclusively to one split, so no recording – and therefore no segment from it – appears in more than one split. Split files are stored in data/splits/train.csv, val.csv and test.csv, each row carrying a recording_id used to enforce isolation.

Recording overlap and file overlap between splits are both zero (verified in results/vardhan_v3_strict_per_segment/aggregate_metrics.json). With 50 segments sampled per file, the test split contains 3,650 segments: 1,000 Background, 1,050 Bebop, 1,050 AR and 550 Phantom.

6. Phantom Receiver Band Shift

Important. In the strict split, all DJI Phantom training files come from the upper-band receiver (H, centre 2.462 GHz), while all Phantom validation and test files come from the lower-band receiver (L, centre 2.422 GHz). A model must therefore recognise the Phantom from a frequency band it has never observed for that class. This is a genuine out-of-distribution shift that a single 40 MHz file cannot bridge, and it explains much of the near-zero Phantom recall under the strict protocol.

The distribution diagnostics, including this band-shift check, can be regenerated with python scripts/analyze_data_distribution.py --splits_dir data/splits --raw_data_dir data/raw/DroneRF.
